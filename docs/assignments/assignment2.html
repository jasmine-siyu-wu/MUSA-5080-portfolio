Assignment 2: Who Do We Know the Least About?

Measuring uncertainty in American Community Survey data

Author

Jasmine Siyu Wu

Published

September 4, 2026

Setup

library(tidyverse)
library(tidycensus)

The question

Every number the American Community Survey publishes is an estimate drawn from a sample, not a count. That means every number comes with a margin of error — a statement about how wrong it might be.

Most people ignore it. In this assignment, you won’t.

In this analysis, I look at median gross rent across counties in California, and ask which communities we measure most and least reliably.

Choosing what to measure

# my_state <- "CA"        # two-letter abbreviation, in quotes
my_state <- params$state

Pulling the data

county_data <- get_acs(

  state = my_state,
  year = 2023,
  survey = "acs5"

How big is the margin of error?

A margin of error of 3,000 means something very different for an estimate of 20,000 than for an estimate of 200,000. To compare across places, we need it as a percentage.

county_data <- county_data %>%
  mutate(moe_pct = moe / estimate * 100)

Now sort to see which counties are measured least reliably.

county_data %>%
  arrange(desc(moe_pct)) %>%
  select(NAME, estimate, moe, moe_pct) %>%
  head(10)

county_data <- county_data %>%

Is the margin of error bigger in small places?

To answer this we need population and our variable side by side, in the same row. That means a wide pull.

county_wide <- get_acs(
  geography = "county",
  variables = c(myvar = my_variable,
                pop = "B01003_001"),
  state = my_state,
  year = 2023,
  survey = "acs5",
  output = "wide"
)

head(county_wide)
# A tibble: 6 × 6
  GEOID NAME                         myvarE myvarM    popE  popM
  <chr> <chr>                         <dbl>  <dbl>   <dbl> <dbl>
1 06001 Alameda County, California     2318     17 1651949    NA
2 06003 Alpine County, California        NA     NA    1695   234
3 06005 Amador County, California      1380    106   41029    NA
4 06007 Butte County, California       1369     31  209470    NA
5 06009 Calaveras County, California   1615    142   45995    NA
6 06011 Colusa County, California      1139     67   21895    NA
county_wide <- county_wide %>%
  mutate(moe_pct = myvarM / myvarE * 100) %>%
  mutate(reliability = case_when(
    moe_pct < 5  ~ "High confidence",
    moe_pct < 15 ~ "Moderate",
    TRUE         ~ "Low confidence"
  ))%>%
  mutate(reliability = factor(reliability, levels = c("Low confidence", "Moderate", "High confidence")))

Now compare the average population of counties in each reliability category.

county_wide %>%
  group_by(reliability) %>%
  summarize(
    n_counties = n(),
    avg_population = mean(popE)
  )
# A tibble: 3 × 3
  reliability     n_counties avg_population
  <fct>                <int>          <dbl>
1 Low confidence           3          6771.
2 Moderate                16         40430.
3 High confidence         39        989118.

The average population of high-confidence counties is near 1 million, while the low-confidence counties have an average population of under 7k. With the moderate-confidence counties having an average population of 40k, the data shows that the higher confidence the estimates are, the more population a county has.

What this means for policy

Across California, we know the least about gross rent in Sierra and Trinity Counties, where the margins of error (MOEs) are as large as 60% of the gross rent estimates. Plumas, Mono, and Mariposa Counties also have MOEs greater than 15% of their estimates. These counties are among the least populous in the state, with an average population of only 6,771, in contrast to California’s largest counties, whose populations approach or exceed one million. This geographic inequality in statistical uncertainty is partly a consequence of the ACS data collection process. Smaller counties generally have smaller samples, resulting in greater uncertainty when sample estimates are used to characterize the county population. In contrast, more populous counties tend to have larger samples and therefore more precise estimates. This unequal certainty also has potential policy implications. Smaller, often more rural counties may have less reliable information available for planning and resource allocation precisely because fewer people live there. If funding formulas or program decisions rely on estimated housing costs, uncertainty in gross rent estimates could affect how local needs are assessed. An underestimated gross rent could understate housing costs and potentially lead to insufficient resources, while an overestimate could overstate local needs. In this sense, statistical uncertainty is not distributed evenly across places, and that unevenness can itself create an equity concern for evidence-based policymaking.

Write A Function and Iterate

pull_state_data <- function(state_abbr) {
  get_acs(
  geography = "county",
  variables = c(myvar = my_variable,
                pop = "B01003_001"),
  state = state_abbr,
  year = 2023,
  survey = "acs5",
  output = "wide"
  ) %>%
  mutate(moe_pct = myvarM / myvarE * 100) %>%
  mutate(reliability = case_when(
    moe_pct < 5  ~ "High confidence",
    moe_pct < 10 ~ "Moderate",
    TRUE         ~ "Low confidence"
  )) %>%
  mutate(reliability = factor(reliability, levels = c("Low confidence", "Moderate", "High confidence"))) %>%
  mutate(state = state_abbr) %>%
  group_by(state, reliability) %>%
  summarize(
    n_counties = n(),
    avg_population = mean(popE)
  )
}

states <- c("PA", "NY", "NJ", "OH")

all_states <- purrr::map_dfr(states, pull_state_data)
